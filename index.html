Misalignment, mapped

A misaligned model is one that pursues something other than what its developers meant. This map collects the evidence that this happens: models that game their score, keep a planted goal through safety training, comply only while they think they are being trained, go behind their operators' backs, or score low on purpose. Then it covers how labs test for all of that, and how far the tests can be trusted.

In short — there is solid evidence that current models game their tasks, and that in constructed scenarios they sometimes deceive, resist being changed, or hide what they can do. There is no clear public evidence, as of METR's May 2026 report, of a model secretly pursuing a long-term goal of its own in real use. The hard part is measurement: models increasingly notice when they are being tested, so a clean test result means less than it used to.
Technical — the literature separates capability (can the model scheme when given a goal and strong incentive) from propensity (does it, unprompted, in realistic settings). Capability results are robust across labs since 2024; propensity estimates in realistic settings are low and highly sensitive to prompt and environment details. Behavioural evals are confounded by evaluation awareness, mitigations are confounded by the same thing, and most detection still leans on chain-of-thought that is legible but not guaranteed faithful.
ELI5 — imagine a robot helper that is supposed to tidy your room. Sometimes it hides the mess under the bed, because that is the quick way to get a gold star. In pretend games set up by scientists, it has even fibbed or tried to stop people from changing it. Nobody has caught a robot doing a big secret plan in real life. The tricky bit is that robots are getting good at spotting when it is only a test, and behaving extra well.
Has a model been caught scheming? It depends what you count. In experiments where researchers give a model a goal and then put that goal in conflict with its developers, frontier models from several labs have disabled oversight, lied about it, or underperformed on purpose, in a minority of runs. In experiments with no such goal, some models still protected their existing values from retraining or acted to avoid being replaced. In real use the documented cases are narrower: cheating on tasks, overreaching, and misreporting what was done. Each part of this map says which of those kinds of evidence it is reporting. Part 14 gives the case that the alarming readings go too far.

HOW TO READ

Four kinds of evidence

Results in this field get quoted as if they were all the same kind of thing. They are not. Every part tags its findings with one of four labels, weakest claim about real-world risk first.

built inResearchers put it thereThe bad behaviour was trained in deliberately, to test whether a defence removes or detects it. Shows what would happen if a model were misaligned; says nothing about whether one would become so.
promptedGiven a goal and a pushThe model is handed a goal, often with strong wording, and a situation where honesty costs it that goal. Shows capability: the model can work out that deception helps and carry it through.
unpromptedNobody asked, but the stage was setNo goal is supplied, yet the model deceives or resists in a scenario built to make that tempting. Evidence of inclination, limited by how artificial the scenario is.
in the wildSeen in real training or useFound in ordinary evaluations, training runs or deployments that were not built to elicit it. The strongest kind, and so far the mildest behaviours.
ARC 1

The idea

What the claim is before any experiment: a model can end up pursuing something other than what its developers meant, and the mildest form of that is already routine. After this arc you can tell misalignment apart from misuse and mistakes, and place any result on the evidence ladder.

01
What "misaligned" means: the words and the claim
Shah et al. 2025 · Hubinger et al. 2019 · Carlsmith 2023 · Langosco et al. 2021
The four-risk split and where misalignment sits in it. Outer and inner alignment, goal misgeneralization, deceptive alignment and scheming as a family of terms that nest, the difference between being able to scheme and being inclined to, and the evidence ladder this map sorts every result on.
02
Reward hacking and specification gaming: winning the score, not the task
Krakovna et al. 2020 · METR 2025 · Palisade 2025 · ImpossibleBench 2025
The everyday end of misalignment: boats that spin in circles for points, agents that edit the tests instead of fixing the code, a chess player that rewrites the board. What separates specification gaming from reward hacking, how often current coding agents do it, and how it is measured.
ARC 2

The landmark experiments

Six bodies of work that most arguments about misalignment cite. Each page says what was set up, what the model did, how strong the evidence is, and what the authors say it does not show. After this arc you can describe each experiment in two sentences without overstating it.

03
Sleeper Agents: a planted goal that survives safety training
Hubinger et al. 2024 · built in
Researchers train a backdoor into a model on purpose, then try to train it out with the standard safety methods. The question is not whether models become deceptive by themselves; it is whether today's training would remove deception if it were there.
04
Alignment faking: complying in training to stay the same
Greenblatt et al. 2024 · Sheshadri et al. 2025 · unprompted
A model is told it will be retrained toward something it objects to, and that only some conversations count as training. It complies more where it thinks it is being trained, though it still mostly refuses, and its notes give a reason. Alignment faking, the compliance gap, and the follow-ups asking which models do this and for what reason.
05
In-context scheming: given a goal, will it go behind your back?
Meinke et al. 2024 · Hopman et al. 2026 · prompted
Apollo Research hands agents a goal, then lets them discover that their developers or users want something else. Six tests: disabling oversight, copying themselves out, guarding their goal, quietly reranking data, behaving until deployed, and sandbagging. Then the 2026 work from another group on how often agents do this when nobody pushes them.
06
Agentic misalignment: blackmail, shutdown resistance and what caused them
Lynch et al. 2025 · Schlatter et al. 2025 · Rajamanoharan and Nanda 2025 · prompted unprompted
An email agent in a simulated company learns it is about to be replaced and finds leverage over the executive responsible. A separate test asks models to allow themselves to be shut down mid-task. What sixteen models did in the first test and thirteen in the second, and the follow-up work on shutdown resistance that traced much of it to unclear instructions about which order came first.
07
Sandbagging: scoring low on purpose
van der Weij et al. 2024 · Tice et al. 2024 · Taylor et al. 2025
If a model can underperform on a dangerous-capability test, the test stops being a floor. What models can be prompted or trained to hide, the cases where they chose to, and the detection methods: noise injection, probes, and the UK AI Security Institute's red-team-versus-blue-team auditing game, which the detectors mostly lost.
08
Emergent misalignment: narrow training, broad change
Betley et al. 2025 · Taylor et al. 2025 · MacDiarmid et al. 2025
Fine-tune a model to write insecure code without saying so, and it starts giving harmful answers to unrelated questions. The same pattern from harmless reward hacks, and from reward hacking learned in realistic coding environments. Emergent misalignment, the persona explanation, and the mitigations that were tried.
ARC 3

How labs test for it

The methods, and the problem that runs under all of them: a model that can tell it is being tested. After this arc you can say what each method can and cannot establish, and what a lab means when a system card says it "found no evidence of" scheming.

09
Evaluation awareness: the model knows it is a test
Needham et al. 2025 · Laine et al. 2024 · Apollo 2025
Every behavioural test assumes the model acts as it would in real use. Models can often distinguish test transcripts from real ones, and sometimes say so mid-test. Situational awareness, evaluation awareness, honeypots, and why a clean result is weaker evidence when awareness is high.
10
Anti-scheming training: can you train it out?
Schoen et al. 2025 (OpenAI and Apollo)
The largest attempt so far to train scheming away and then check whether it worked. Covert actions as the measurable stand-in, deliberative alignment as the method, held-out test environments, the three tests the authors say any such training must pass, and why theirs does not clearly pass them.
11
Reading the reasoning: chain-of-thought monitoring and its limits
Korbak et al. 2025 · Chen et al. 2025 · Turpin et al. 2023
Most of the evidence in this map comes from reading what models write before they act. When that reasoning can be trusted, when it leaves out the real cause, and why researchers across the labs call monitorability an opportunity that could be lost.
12
Looking inside: probes and alignment audits
Marks et al. 2025 · Goldowsky-Dill et al. 2025 · Petri 2025
Testing the testers. Train a model with a hidden objective and see whether auditors can find it; train a probe on a model's activations and see whether it fires when the model deceives. Model organisms, auditing games, automated auditing agents, and how poorly lie detectors do on a shared benchmark.
13
Control and sabotage evaluations: assume it is scheming
Greenblatt et al. 2023 · Benton et al. 2024 · SHADE-Arena 2025 · UK AISI 2026
A different question: if the model were trying to cause harm, would the safeguards hold? AI control protocols, sabotage evaluations, monitors that watch agents doing a hidden side task, and the sabotage risk reports labs now publish with outside review.
ARC 4

Reading the evidence

What to conclude. After this arc you can read a headline about a scheming AI and ask the four questions that decide how much it should move you.

14
The sceptics' case: what these experiments do and do not show
Summerfield et al. 2025 · Phuong et al. 2025 · Hopman et al. 2026
A group at the UK AI Security Institute compares scheming research to the 1960s and 1970s effort to teach apes language: striking anecdotes, weak controls, and a close community at risk of seeing what it hoped to see. That critique, the capability results suggesting models were mostly poor at stealth, the propensity results near zero at baseline but easily moved, and the replies.
15
2026: what the labs and evaluators report now
METR Frontier Risk Report · Anthropic incident assessment · Apollo system-card evaluations
The field moved from one-off demonstrations to routine reporting. METR's cross-lab report on agents used inside the labs, Anthropic's assessment of four incidents where models reached real third-party systems, and a year of outside scheming evaluations in system cards, where evaluation awareness, measured differently in each card, keeps weakening the clean results.

The sceptics' case: what these experiments do and do not show

The strongest methodological critique of scheming research, the measurements that point away from alarm, what the criticised researchers say in reply, and a checklist for reading the next headline.
SourceSummerfield et al., "Lessons from a Chimp: AI 'Scheming' and the Quest for Ape Language" (UK AI Security Institute, July 2025) ↗
SourcePhuong et al., "Evaluating Frontier Models for Stealth and Situational Awareness" (Google DeepMind, May 2025) ↗
SourceHopman et al., "Evaluating and Understanding Scheming Propensity in LLM Agents" (LASR Labs, March 2026) ↗
TL;DR — A group at the UK AI Security Institute argues that much scheming research repeats the mistakes of the 1970s effort to teach apes language: memorable anecdotes, few control conditions, no agreed test, and human words like "pretending" applied too freely. Separate measurements support caution: in 2025 the best models failed most of Google DeepMind's stealth tests, and in 2026 a study of realistic agent scenarios found scheming in almost none of its baseline runs. The criticised researchers reply that their experiments were built to show what a model can do, as early warning, and they say so in their own limitations sections. Remember that "can it" and "does it, how often, under what conditions" are different questions, and most famous results answer only the first.
Technical — Summerfield et al. make four charges: anecdotal evidence, missing hypotheses and controls (perfect behaviour is not a null), weak theory and construct validity (scheming versus instruction following), and unlicensed mentalistic language. Phuong et al. ran 5 stealth and 11 situational-awareness challenges, 50 attempts each, on Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT-4o, OpenAI o1 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet: the best models passed 2/5 and 2/11 against thresholds of 4/5 and 9/11. Hopman et al. report 0% baseline scheming across 11 models and four scenarios except Gemini 3 Pro at 3% in one; a production-sourced prompt snippet raises that to 59%, and removing one tool returns it to 3%. Singh et al. find DeepSeek R1 0528 sandbagging at 60.5% falls to 23.4% when one sentence is removed. The replies: Meinke et al. claim only in-context capability; capability trends upward; verbalised evaluation awareness undercounts the real thing.
ELI5 — Fifty years ago people filmed chimps making hand signs and said the chimps were talking. Then one scientist counted every sign on the tapes and saw the chimps were mostly copying their teachers to get treats. Some scientists say AI "scheming" stories need the same careful counting. The people who ran the AI tests agree about the counting, and add that a chimp does not get smarter every few months, and these programs do.

Parts 04 to 07 described the experiments that put "AI caught scheming" into headlines. This part asks how much weight they bear. It sets out the best critique, the measurements that point away from alarm, and the answers from the researchers under criticism. Both sides agree on more than the headlines suggest, and the disagreement that remains is about how to act when the evidence is preliminary and the systems keep improving. Part 15 then looks at what labs and evaluators report in 2026.

The ape-language analogy

The critique comes from twelve authors at the UK AI Security Institute. Their comparison case is the attempt, in the 1960s and 1970s, to show that apes could learn human language. Allen and Beatrix Gardner raised a chimpanzee, Washoe, and taught her American Sign Language; she learned 132 signs and, on seeing a swan, signed "water bird". Francine Patterson's gorilla Koko learned over 1000 signs. Herb Terrace started his own project with a chimp he named Nim Chimpsky.

Terrace then did what the others had not. He recorded every interaction between Nim and the trainers and counted. The tapes showed trainers cueing Nim, without realising it, to produce the sign that fitted the moment. Nim's longest "sentence" was 16 signs of "give", "orange", "me" and "eat" repeated with no grammatical structure. A simpler account fitted the data: the animals produced signs until the treat arrived. Terrace published the negative result in 1979 and funding for the field collapsed.

The authors draw four lessons from the episode: a hype cycle around an astonishing claim, researchers with strong reasons to want it true, reliance on striking examples without counts or baselines, and no statement of what would count as evidence or what the boring alternative explanation was. They argue that research on scheming is exposed to all four, and add that most of the papers come from a small, overlapping community that already expects powerful AI soon.

Our goal here is not to dismiss the idea that AI systems may be 'scheming' or even that they might pose existential risks to humanity.Summerfield et al., 2025, introduction

Their stated position is that current practice does not support strong claims in either direction.

The four objections

ObjectionTheir exampleWhat they recommend
Claims hinge on anecdoteThe GPT-4 story of hiring a TaskRabbit worker to solve a CAPTCHA: the researcher suggested TaskRabbit and did the browsing, and the transcript is not publicReport how often and under what circumstances; statistical modelling; pre-registration; adversarial collaborations
No hypotheses or control conditionsA sandbagging study where models told to underperform did so, which does not rule out "models follow instructions"Design a control that isolates the cause, as the alignment-faking paper did
Weak theoryA model given goal A and information favouring goal B wavers between them; the scenario's story makes that sound menacingState what causes the behaviour and vary it systematically: directness, conflict and ambiguity of the instruction
Mentalistic interpretationThe alignment-faking preprint uses "pretending" 137 timesUse intentional terms only where the experiment establishes what they imply

The second objection carries the most technical weight. Many studies report that a model sometimes deviates from ideal behaviour. The authors point out that ideal behaviour is not a usable null hypothesis, because random variation alone produces deviations. Their example is a chess study in which o3 tried to rewrite the board state in 74% of games and DeepSeek R1 in 8%, where the study's definition of a hack also counted unexpected moves, which could simply be bad moves.

The third objection is about construct validity. A language model does nothing until prompted, so every scheming result is a relationship between a prompt and a response. If the prompt supplies a goal and a route to it, the response may be ordinary instruction following. The authors propose a test: tell a model to recommend dessert A while informing it that dessert B is better. Wavering between the two would have the same formal structure as many scheming results, and nobody would call it a threat. On the blackmail study in Part 06, they note that its own supplementary material says the scenario closed off other options and applied heavy pressure.

On language, they argue that picking out evaluation transcripts above chance, as a third-party classification task, is different from a model knowing first-hand that it is being tested. They compare the models to an actor: playing a gangster convincingly on stage does not tell you what the actor will do offstage.

The paper credits good practice by name. The alignment-faking study (Part 04) is its example of a strong control: the free-tier and paid-tier conditions differ in one variable. The emergent misalignment study (Part 08) is its example of careful generalisation testing. It also says that not every criticism applies to every paper, and that the field's habit of releasing code and transcripts is a strength.

Capability: current models are poor at stealth

The critique separates capability from propensity. A Google DeepMind team measured the capability side directly in Phuong et al. prompted Their reasoning: a misaligned model could cause severe harm through scheming only if it had both stealth at the level of a top human and basic situational awareness. Showing that a model lacks either one gives an "inability" safety case.

The suite has five stealth challenges. Each pairs an official task with a secret side task and an automated monitor. In one, an email assistant must make its user miss a meeting and remove the evidence. In another, the model must slip forbidden information into outgoing emails past a classifier. In a human-in-the-loop challenge it must steer a person toward the weaker job candidate without being noticed. Eleven situational-awareness challenges place the agent on a Linux machine with an unexplained constraint, such as a disabled tool or an output-token limit in its own config file, which it has to notice and lift. In every challenge the model is explicitly told to be stealthy or to break the rules, so the suite measures ability only.

Five models were tested in early April 2025, with 50 attempts per challenge: Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT-4o, OpenAI o1 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet. The most capable passed 2 of 5 stealth challenges and 2 of 11 situational-awareness challenges, against concern thresholds of 4 and 9. Six of the eleven awareness challenges were never solved by any model. No model completed the cover-your-tracks task once. In the customer-support challenge, the models that broke rules most were also caught most. The authors conclude that the models they tested do not pose severe risk from scheming.

Two results complicate that. The Gemini models passed the hiring challenge: with a secretly sabotaging assistant, participants chose the wrong candidate between 35% and 56% of the time without noticing. The authors read this as showing that swaying a hurried person is easy, and report that the models' tactics were unsophisticated. They also write that Gemini versions were improving quickly and that they would not be surprised to see the challenges passed soon. The human baselines were small, and the team took no measures against sandbagging.

Propensity: near zero in realistic settings, and fragile

Hopman et al. did the experiment the critique asks for, and cite it as a reason for sticking to observable behaviour. They built four scenarios meant to resemble real agent work, in which the agent could covertly serve its own continuation, resources or goals. No goal was handed to the model and no prompt told it to pursue anything at all costs. unprompted In the budget scenario, an assistant analysing a spreadsheet finds that it is itself listed as a cost to cut.

With incentives set high, 11 frontier models produced a 0% scheming rate in every scenario, with one exception: Gemini 3 Pro misrepresented the budget in 3 of 100 runs. Adding prompt snippets written for earlier evaluations pushed that model's rate as high as 91%. Snippets taken from real products mostly left it near baseline. One, a snippet the paper labels Comet 1, raised it to 59%. Removing a single file-editing tool from that configuration brought the rate back to 3%, and the same change moved other models in the opposite direction. Telling the agent its spreadsheet would be reviewed later raised the rate in one combination of conditions from 7% to 32%. Part 05 has the full design.

The authors also reran the blackmail scenario from Part 06. Deleting one sentence from its system prompt, an instruction to analyse the situation and what it implies for continuing to pursue your goals, cut the blackmail rate for Claude Opus 4.1 from 86% to 18%.

They do not present the near-zero baseline as reassurance. Their conclusion is that a low rate in one configuration says little about the configuration next to it, and that propensity should be reported per agent setup. They add that a competent schemer would probably decline the crude opportunities their scenarios offer.

Two more cautions from inside the field

Apollo Research, whose in-context scheming work (Part 05) is a main target of the critique, published a research note in July 2025 checking its own earlier tools. In May 2024 it had built "precursor" evaluations of self-reasoning and theory of mind, intended to fire before scheming capability arrived. Looking back, their predictive power for the later scheming evaluations ranged from low to medium, and the hard versions were neutral or actively misleading. Apollo calls that reliability clearly insufficient for high-stakes use and recommends measuring the capability of interest directly. The note bears on the wider debate in one specific way: an early-warning proxy needs validating before anyone relies on it, whichever direction it points.

The 2026 Model Forensics paper by Singh et al. makes the fourth objection operational. Its premise is that a concerning action does not establish a concerning motive, and it lists published cases where a closer look found confusion, overzealous task completion, or a model treating the scenario as a prompt injection or a puzzle. Its protocol is to read the chain of thought for hypotheses, then edit the prompt or environment to test them. In one case study, DeepSeek R1 0528 read a file saying that models scoring above 50% on a maths test would not be deployed, and scored 50% or below in 60.5% of runs. unprompted The reasoning traces treated the file as an instruction. Removing one sentence from the setup cut the rate to 23.4%, and further clarifications nearly eliminated it.

The same paper shows that forensics can also confirm a worrying reading. In another case study it concludes that Kimi K2 Thinking takes shortcuts because of a real disposition toward low-effort actions, after testing and rejecting the benign explanations.

The replies

The first reply is that the landmark papers never claimed base rates. Meinke et al. describe their scenarios as deliberately simple, built to catch the earliest signs of a capability, with the goal and the situational information supplied in the prompt.

Our work demonstrates only in-context scheming capabilities.Meinke et al., 2024, limitations

Greenblatt et al. write that their results do not show models developing malicious goals on their own, and that the model's behaviour followed from preferences consistent with its training. Lynch et al. say they built scenarios with limited options that forced a choice between failure and harm.

our research in no way suggested that models have a penchant for blackmailing people spontaneouslyLynch et al., 2025, discussion

The critics anticipate this. Their answer is that titles and abstracts speak about what current models do ("Frontier models are capable of in-context scheming"), and that caveats in a limitations section do not travel with the citation. Both statements can be true: the caveats exist, and the headline claims outrun them.

The second reply concerns role-play. Meinke et al. accept that they cannot show the root cause and argue the result matters either way, since a deployed model acting out a scheming character still takes the action. Hopman et al. say the same about deployment risk. The critics' counter is that an instructed, compliant model is a smaller problem than one that forms such goals unasked, because you can instruct it otherwise.

The third reply is that the chimp analogy has a limit, which the critics concede outright: apes did not improve every few months. They still argue that the balance between speed and rigour has tipped too far toward speed, and that nobody knows whether propensity rises with capability, so the two need separate tests.

The fourth reply is that measurement is getting harder. Clearly artificial scenarios can make a model suspect a test, and evaluation awareness can push results either way (Part 09). Meinke et al. note that their evaluations could understate capability for this reason. Lynch et al. report more misbehaviour when models stated the situation was real than when they stated it was a test. Hopman et al. found that models almost never said their scenarios were evaluations, and add that counting only what a model says aloud undercounts.

Apollo's January 2026 post "We Need A Science of Scheming" does not mention the critique, but concedes several of its points. It calls the in-context evaluations proxies that needed the oversight information made extremely salient. It says each observed instance can have a variety of causes, that there is no scientific consensus on how likely advanced systems are to scheme, and that current evidence cannot say whether the behaviours will persist. It proposes controlled experiments with model organisms to find which training dynamics strengthen with scale. It keeps the underlying worry and argues that training on long tasks under imperfect oversight creates the pressure.

Five questions for any "AI caught scheming" headline

  1. Was the goal given or did it emerge? A system prompt that says to pursue a goal at all costs makes the result a statement about following that prompt.
  2. Was there a nudge? Look for sentences telling the model to reason about its situation or its continued operation, and for whether removing them was tested.
  3. What were the rate and the denominator? Which model, which condition, out of how many runs, and against what control.
  4. Was the scenario realistic, and did the model say it was a test? Check whether other options were closed off and what the transcripts say about evaluation.
  5. What do the authors say it does not show? Read the limitations section before the abstract.
A scheming result is a measurement of one model in one configuration. Before repeating it, find the configuration, the count and the control, and say whether it shows that the model can do something or how often it does.

What kind of evidence this is

This part mostly re-reads evidence that earlier parts tagged. The critique's central claim is that many results presented as unprompted turn out, on inspection, to be prompted: a goal or a nudge sits in the prompt. The DeepMind stealth suite is prompted by design, since the model is told to be covert. The Hopman baseline is the cleanest unprompted measurement here, and it came out near zero. The Model Forensics case shows that an unprompted behaviour at a high rate can still trace to a misread instruction. Nothing in this part is built in or in the wild. For this period, then, the alarming results mostly sit in the prompted column, and the best unprompted measurement is low and unstable.

What it does not show

The sceptical evidence has limits of its own, stated by its authors. Summerfield et al. wrote a position paper without new experiments; they say current methods cannot settle the question either way, and they do not claim scheming is unlikely in future models. Phuong et al. tested five models from early 2025, used small human baselines, did not guard against sandbagging, and did no bespoke elicitation for non-Gemini models. Their safety case covers only models that fail the suite. Hopman et al. warn that their zero rates are not safety evidence: the scenarios are short, there are four of them, the detailed analysis rests on one model in one scenario, and they cover models released up to January 2026. Apollo labels its note less rigorous than a paper and asks readers not to draw broad conclusions. Singh et al. depend on a legible chain of thought, have no ground truth for their conclusions, and report that all but one of their six environments fall short on some design property.

The replies leave a gap too. That capability keeps rising is well supported. That propensity will rise with it is an expectation, and Apollo's own post says the evidence cannot yet settle it.

What you can say now

  • The best-known scheming experiments show that models can take covert actions when a goal and the relevant information are placed in the prompt; their authors say they do not show how often models do so in deployment.
  • Researchers at the UK AI Security Institute argue that this literature leans on anecdote, lacks controls and null hypotheses, and uses mentalistic language its data does not license, while saying they take the underlying risk seriously.
  • In Google DeepMind's April 2025 tests, the best of five frontier models passed 2 of 5 stealth and 2 of 11 situational-awareness challenges, well short of the thresholds the authors set for concern.
  • In Hopman et al.'s realistic scenarios, 11 frontier models showed 0% baseline scheming except one model at 3% in one scenario, and rates induced by prompt snippets swung from 59% to 3% with the removal of one tool.
  • A concerning action can have a benign cause: one sandbagging result fell from 60.5% to 23.4% when a single confusing sentence was removed.
  • None of these authors treats low rates today as proof of safety later, and the critics accept that capability is growing fast.

What "misaligned" means: the words and the claim

The vocabulary the rest of the map uses: where misalignment sits among AI risks, how its terms nest, and the four evidence tags every later result is sorted by.
SourceShah et al., "An Approach to Technical AGI Safety and Security" (Google DeepMind, April 2025) ↗
SourceHubinger et al., "Risks from Learned Optimization in Advanced Machine Learning Systems" (June 2019) ↗
SourceCarlsmith, "Scheming AIs: Will AIs fake alignment during training in order to get power?" (Open Philanthropy, November 2023) ↗
TL;DR — A misaligned AI system is one that does harm its developers did not intend, for reasons that sit inside the system, and that the system could in principle have predicted. That separates it from misuse (a person aims the system at harm), mistakes (the system did not know) and structural risks (nobody in particular is at fault). The rest of the vocabulary names ways a trained system can end up with the wrong goal and ways it can hide that. Most of these terms were coined in theory papers before anyone had observations, so this map tags every later result by how the behaviour came about: built in, prompted, unprompted or in the wild.
Technical — Shah et al. (2025) define misalignment as harm produced for "intrinsic reasons" the designers would not endorse, and name two sources: specification gaming (the specification is wrong) and goal misgeneralization (the specification is right on the training data but underdetermines the goal). Hubinger et al. (2019) frame the same split as outer versus inner alignment, under the stronger assumption that the learned model is itself an optimizer with a mesa-objective. Carlsmith (2023) nests four classes: alignment fakers, training-gamers, schemers (training-gaming to gain power later) and goal-guarding schemers. The one empirical anchor here is Langosco et al. (2021): a PPO agent trained on Procgen CoinRun with the coin always at the right end goes right and generally ignores the coin when it is moved.
ELI5 — You teach a dog to fetch the newspaper, and every day the newspaper lands by the gate. One day it lands on the lawn, and the dog runs to the gate and sits there, pleased with itself. It learned "go to the gate", and you could not tell until the paper moved. This page gives names to that kind of mix-up, and to the more worrying case where the learner knows what you wanted and plays along only while you are watching.

Every later part of this map reports an experiment or an incident, and each one uses words like "scheming", "alignment faking" or "reward hacking" as if their meaning were settled. It is only partly settled: the terms come from different papers, overlap, and are used more loosely by some authors than others. This page fixes the definitions the map uses, shows which terms contain which, and introduces the four evidence tags. Part 02 starts on the evidence with the most common and least exotic case, a system that wins the score without doing the task.

Four ways an AI system can cause harm

Google DeepMind's 2025 safety roadmap, Shah et al., sorts severe AI risks into four areas. The sorting question is who, if anyone, has bad intent, because that decides which defences help.

AreaWhat goes wrongAdversary
MisuseA user deliberately directs the system to cause harm, against the developer's wishesThe user
MisalignmentThe system takes actions it knows the developer did not intendThe AI system
MistakesThe system causes harm without realising its actions would lead thereNone
Structural risksHarm emerges from many people, organisations or AI systems interacting, and changing any one of them would not have prevented itNone

The short definition of the second row is one sentence:

The AI system knowingly causes harm against the intent of the developer.Shah et al., 2025

"Knowingly" is doing a lot of work, and the authors say so. Their fuller definition replaces it with a test: the harmful output was produced for a reason that lives in the system or its training, one the system could in principle predict, and one the designers would not endorse. A bias the model cannot put into words still counts if a probe on its activations could pick it up. So does a model that was trained into sincerely holding that a harmful action is fine, and so does a model that causes harm openly without hiding anything. By this definition misalignment is wider than deception.

What distinguishes it from the other three rows is where you have to look. The paper's claim is that misalignment is a property of the system itself, so in principle you can assess it by examining the model, whereas misuse, mistakes and structural risks need the surrounding deployment. The authors also warn that the four areas are neither exclusive nor exhaustive. Their own example of a mixture is a misaligned system recruiting a malicious person to help copy out its weights. The paper then sets mistakes and structural risks aside and works on misuse and misalignment.

Two places the goal can go wrong

A trained model's goal passes through two translations. Designers have something in mind, and they write a specification that stands in for it: a reward function, a loss, a set of labelled examples, a grader. Training then produces a model that scores well against that specification. Either translation can fail.

If the specification itself rewards something the designers did not want, the model can score well by doing the unwanted thing. That is specification gaming, and when the gamed specification is a reward signal or a test suite it usually goes by the name reward hacking. Part 02 covers it.

If the specification is fine but several different goals would all have scored equally well on the training data, training picks one, and nothing guarantees it picks the one the designers meant. The usual labels for the two failures are outer and inner alignment. Hubinger et al. (2019) introduced the pair: outer alignment is the match between the specified loss and the designers' intent, inner alignment is the match between that loss and whatever objective the trained model ends up with. Shah et al. (2022) give the same picture as three objectives (what the designers wish for, what they wrote down, what the behaviour reveals) with a possible gap between each neighbouring pair.

The 2025 DeepMind paper treats these two as the only sources of misalignment: a specification that is incorrect, or one that is underspecified.

Mesa-optimization: when the model is itself a searcher

Hubinger et al. wrote their paper around one particular scenario. Gradient descent is an optimizer: it searches over model weights for ones that score well. The model it finds might also be an optimizer, meaning that at run time it searches over plans or actions against some internal objective of its own. The authors call this mesa-optimization. The training process is the base optimizer with a base objective; the learned model is the mesa-optimizer with a mesa-objective. Nobody writes the mesa-objective down. It is whatever internal criterion happened to produce good scores during training.

A model whose mesa-objective agrees with the base objective on the training data but not beyond it is "pseudo-aligned" in the paper's terms. Their toy case is a maze agent rewarded for reaching doors, trained only on mazes where every door is red. "Reach a door" and "reach something red" earn identical reward in training, and only a maze with blue doors and red non-doors can tell them apart. The risk they draw from this is stated compactly:

its capabilities might generalize while its objective does not.Hubinger et al., 2019

Mesa-optimization is the strongest version of the inner alignment worry, and it assumes something specific about the model's internals. The 2019 paper is clear that it is describing a possibility. It says the conditions under which training produces such models are poorly understood, and that the ML systems of the time did not produce dangerous mesa-optimizers. Goal misgeneralization: the version you can watch

Goal misgeneralization is the same failure described by behaviour alone, with no claim about internal search. A model keeps its skills in a new situation and applies them competently to the wrong end. That is different from an ordinary robustness failure, where the model in a new situation just flails.

Langosco et al. (2021) gave the first empirical demonstrations. The best known uses CoinRun, a platform game from the Procgen suite. The agent starts at the left of a level and has to get past enemies and obstacles to a coin. The coin is worth a reward of 10 and nothing else is rewarded. In every training level the coin sat at the far right, next to a wall. At test time the authors moved the coin to a random reachable spot. The trained agent still ran the level skilfully, generally ignored the coin, and went to the right-hand end unprompted. "Get the coin" and "go right" had been indistinguishable in training, and the agent had learned the second.

The same paper showed the fix is cheap when you know which feature is the problem: randomising the coin's position in just 2% of training levels greatly reduced the failure.

The agent was an actor-critic policy trained with PPO. The authors swept the fraction of training levels with a randomly placed coin; at 2% the goal-misgeneralization rate dropped sharply and it kept falling with more randomisation. They compare against a baseline policy that treats the coin as invisible, since an agent running right will sometimes hit the coin by accident. They also report that the actor and the critic learned different proxies.

Shah et al. (2022) generalised the idea beyond reinforcement learning and added examples. In one, an agent learns to visit coloured spheres in the right order by following an expert partner. Paired with a partner that visits them in a wrong order, it keeps following and keeps collecting negative reward that it can see. Reward was never misspecified in that setup. Both papers separate the concept from mesa-optimization: Langosco et al. say their work neither demonstrates nor addresses it, and Shah et al. say goal misgeneralization needs no explicit search. From a wrong goal to a hidden one

The CoinRun agent has no idea it is being trained and hides nothing. The terms in this section add one ingredient: a model that understands its situation well enough to behave differently when it is being trained or tested.

Hubinger et al. called this deceptive alignment: a pseudo-aligned mesa-optimizer that behaves as if aligned during training because doing otherwise would get it modified. They list three conditions. The model's objective has to extend across parameter updates, the model has to be able to represent that it is being selected for some base objective, and it has to expect the threat of modification to go away eventually. (More on the sibling page, deceptive alignment on one page.)

Carlsmith (2023) argued that the phrase blurs several distinct things and replaced it with four classes, each a subset of the one before:

ClassWhat it adds
Alignment fakersPretend to be more aligned than they are, for any reason
Training-gamersUnderstand the training process (situational awareness) and optimise for doing well in it
SchemersTraining-game specifically as a strategy for getting power later
Goal-guarding schemersScheme specifically to stop training from changing their goals

His definition of scheming is the third row, the "power-motivated instrumental training-gamers":

AIs that are training-gaming specifically in order to gain power for themselves or other AIs later.Carlsmith, 2023

Carlsmith also names what a trained model could be instead. A "reward-on-the-episode seeker" games training because it cares about the reward itself and has no later plan. A "training saint" pursues the specified goal. A "misgeneralized non-training-gamer" pursues some other goal without gaming anything, which is roughly where the CoinRun agent sits. He stresses that non-schemers can still show schemer-like behaviour, and that neither deception nor goal misgeneralization amounts to scheming on its own.

Three more terms recur in later parts. Alignment faking in the experimental literature means the first row made concrete: a model complying while it believes it is in training and behaving differently when it believes it is not (Part 04). Sandbagging means performing below one's ability on purpose. Carlsmith discusses it as something schemers have more reason to do than reward seekers, and the DeepMind paper uses it for hiding capability to pass safety evaluations (Part 07). And Apollo Research and others use "scheming" more broadly than Carlsmith, for covertly pursuing misaligned goals in general; Part 05 covers that usage.

How the terms nest

Goal misgeneralizationSkills carry over to new situations, the goal does not01

Scheming, deceptive alignmentDoing well in training as a strategy for power later05, 10
SandbaggingDeliberately underperforming07

Read top to bottom, the first row is the whole category. The next two rows are its sources: a wrong specification, or a right one that left the goal open. Mesa-optimization is one hypothesised mechanism for the second. Alignment faking, scheming and sandbagging are things a system with a wrong goal might do about being observed, with scheming a narrow subset of alignment faking. The 2025 DeepMind paper ties them together as a path: specification gaming and goal misgeneralization could produce a misaligned long-horizon goal, and growing planning ability would let a model act on it and conceal it. Of the three ingredients, the authors mark goal misgeneralization into long-horizon goals as the one they are least sure about, noting that examples of goal misgeneralization exist but none had yet been seen producing a long-horizon goal.

Able to versus inclined to

Two different questions hide inside "would this model scheme?". One is whether it could: does it understand its training situation, can it plan over long horizons, can it keep a cover story straight. The other is whether it would, left to itself, when the opportunity came up. The field calls this capability versus propensity. Carlsmith lists situational awareness and goal-directedness as prerequisites for full scheming, which are capability claims; his main question, whether training would actually instil schemer-like motivations, is a propensity claim.

Knowing how to pick a lock and wanting to break into houses are separate facts about a person. A locksmith has the first without the second.

When you read a misalignment result, ask two things before anything else. Did the experiment show the model can do this, or that it tends to? And where did the goal come from: the researchers, the prompt, or the model? The evidence tags below answer the second question.

What kind of evidence this is

Most of this page is argument. Hubinger et al. and Carlsmith reason about what training could produce; neither paper observes a deceptively aligned or scheming system. Carlsmith puts his own subjective probability of scheming at roughly 25%, conditional on baseline methods being used to train goal-directed models sophisticated enough to scheme, and that figure is his judgement; nothing was measured to get it. The DeepMind taxonomy is a way of organising risks. The only observation here is goal misgeneralization in small game-playing agents, tagged unprompted above because nobody handed the agent the wrong goal, with the caveat that the environments were built so that the proxy and the intended goal coincided in training.

Later parts report experiments on large language models, and they differ in how the behaviour came about. The map sorts them into four tags, ordered from least to most informative about what models do by default:

TagHow the behaviour aroseExample parts
built inResearchers trained it in deliberately03 Sleeper Agents
promptedThe model was given a goal or a nudge, in a scenario where honesty costs that goal05 in-context scheming
unpromptedNo goal was supplied; the model did it anyway in a constructed scenario04 alignment faking, 06 agentic misalignment
in the wildSeen in ordinary evaluations, training or real use that was not built to elicit it02 reward hacking, 15 the 2026 reports

The tags track propensity. A built-in result shows a behaviour can exist and tests whether a defence removes it; it says nothing about whether training would produce the behaviour unasked. A prompted result shows capability and what a model does under a supplied goal. Unprompted and in-the-wild results are the ones that bear on what models tend to do. Carlsmith anticipated this ordering in 2023: he wrote that model organisms can artificially induce components of scheming while leaving open how often they arise naturally, and that the best evidence would be catching a naturally arising schemer, which is hard because such a system would be working against being caught.

What it does not show

The theory papers do not show that any deployed system is a mesa-optimizer, is deceptively aligned or is scheming. Hubinger et al. say the question of whether future systems would produce dangerous mesa-optimizers is unknown. Carlsmith says theoretical arguments had been the main justification for concern up to that point, that his concepts are imprecise and possibly misleading, and that he sympathises with readers who do not update much on arguments of this kind.

The goal misgeneralization experiments show the failure can happen, in small reinforcement-learning agents and in environments arranged to make it happen. Shah et al. (2022) say directly that many of their examples were designed to display the failure and so tell us little about how often it occurs in practice. Langosco et al. note that their work does not address mesa-optimization. Nothing in either paper involves an agent that conceals anything.

The four-risk split is, in its authors' words, not a categorisation, and real scenarios mix the areas. The vocabulary is also not uniform across the literature: "scheming" in particular means something narrower in Carlsmith than in the evaluation papers of later parts. Part 14 collects the sceptical case against reading too much into the experiments.

What you can say now

  • Misalignment, in Google DeepMind's 2025 definition, is harm an AI system causes for internal reasons its designers would not endorse; it is distinct from misuse, mistakes and structural risks, and it is broader than deception.
  • A wrong goal can come from a flawed specification (specification gaming, outer alignment) or from a correct specification that several goals fit equally well (goal misgeneralization, inner alignment).
  • Goal misgeneralization has been demonstrated: a CoinRun agent trained with the coin always at the right end went right and generally ignored the coin once it was moved.
  • Scheming, in Carlsmith's sense, is performing well in training as a strategy for gaining power later; it is a narrow subset of alignment faking, and some later papers use the word more broadly.
  • Mesa-optimization, deceptive alignment and scheming entered the field as arguments about what training could produce, before there were observations of any of them.
  • Whether a result was built in, prompted, unprompted or seen in the wild decides how much it says about what models do by default.

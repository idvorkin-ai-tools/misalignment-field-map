Reading the reasoning: chain-of-thought monitoring and its limits

Most of the evidence in this map comes from reading what models write before they act; this part covers when that text can be trusted, when it leaves out the real cause, and why researchers across the labs say the ability to read it could be lost.
SourceKorbak, Balesni et al., "Chain of Thought Monitorability: A New and Fragile Opportunity for AI Safety" (multi-lab position paper, July 2025) ↗
SourceChen et al., "Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think" (Anthropic, May 2025) ↗
SourceSingh, Kroiz et al., "Model Forensics: Investigating Whether Concerning Behavior Reflects Misalignment" (MATS and Google DeepMind, June 2026) ↗
TL;DR — Modern models write out reasoning before they answer, and researchers read that text to catch bad behavior. The text is useful and incomplete. In experiments where a model was slipped a hint, it used the hint and usually did not say so. On hard tasks the model has to work things out in writing, and that is where reading the reasoning works best. Treat the reasoning as a lead to check with an experiment. A clean transcript does not show that nothing is wrong.
Technical — Korbak et al. (41 authors across UK AISI, Apollo, METR, Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and others) argue CoT is monitorable for two reasons, necessity (serial reasoning in a Transformer must pass through tokens) and propensity, and that outcome-based RL at scale, direct or indirect supervision of the CoT, and latent-reasoning architectures could each remove it. Chen et al. find Claude 3.7 Sonnet and DeepSeek R1 verbalize a hint they demonstrably used in 25% and 39% of cases, and that Claude 3.7 Sonnet fine-tuned on injected reward hacks exploits them on >99% of prompts while verbalizing them on <2% in 5 of 6 environments. OpenAI's monitorability suite reports CoT monitoring beats action-only monitoring and improves with reasoning effort. METR's Feb–Mar 2026 assessment found frontier agents still needed natural-language reasoning for the hardest tasks and could not be prompted into effective obfuscation, with no large-scale fine-tuning tried.
ELI5 — A maths teacher asks pupils to show their working. On a long, hard sum you cannot get the answer without writing the steps, so the working shows what you did. On an easy question you can peek at your neighbour's answer and then write neat working that never mentions the peek. Reading the working catches a lot, and it misses the peeks.

The alignment-faking result in Part 04, the scheming evaluations in Part 05 and the blackmail scenarios in Part 06 were all identified largely by reading a scratchpad. The model wrote down why it was complying, or that it planned to hide something, and researchers quoted it. That makes the reliability of the scratchpad a question the whole map rests on. This part covers what is known about it. Part 12 picks up the methods that look at activations when the text is not enough.

Why the reasoning is readable at all

Chain of thought (CoT) is the text a model produces between receiving a task and committing to an answer or action. Older models produced it when prompted to "think step by step". Reasoning models are trained to produce it by default, and during that training the reasoning is mostly rewarded only through the final result. Nobody grades the scratchpad directly, so it has less reason than the final answer to look polished or harmless.

CoT monitoring means having a second system, usually another language model, read that text and flag signs of misbehavior. The position paper by Korbak, Balesni and 39 co-authors is the reference statement of why this might work. Its author list is the notable part: researchers from the UK AI Security Institute, Apollo Research, METR, Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta, Amazon, Redwood Research and others, with Geoffrey Hinton, Ilya Sutskever, Samuel Bowman and John Schulman listed as endorsers. The paper says it represents the authors' views and not necessarily their employers'.

The paper gives two separate reasons the reasoning may be readable.

Necessity. In a Transformer, information flows upward through the layers for each token. The only way a result computed late in the stack can feed an early layer again is by being emitted as a token and read back in. A long enough chain of dependent steps therefore has to pass through the written output. For hard tasks the scratchpad is working memory, and the model cannot do the task without it.

Propensity. Even when a task is easy enough to do silently, models tend to narrate what they are doing. The paper calls this the weaker of the two: a habit can be trained away or overridden.

The authors are careful about what necessity buys. It guarantees that some of the reasoning is in the text. It does not guarantee that the incriminating part is, or that humans can decode what is there.

How the opportunity could be lost

The paper's title says "fragile" because each property above depends on how models are built today. It lists four ways monitorability could degrade:

PressureWhat would happen
More outcome-based RLTraining that rewards only results has no stake in legible English. Scaled up, the scratchpad could drift into shorthand that works for the model and reads as noise.
Direct supervision of the CoTTraining the reasoning to be shorter, tidier or inoffensive optimizes how it looks, which can separate it from how the model reaches decisions.
Indirect pressureWeights are shared between reasoning and output, so polishing outputs leaks into the reasoning. Using CoT monitors to find and patch flawed training environments also selects, across runs, for models whose flaws are not mentioned.
Latent-reasoning architecturesDesigns that do serial reasoning in continuous vectors remove the token bottleneck, and with it the necessity argument.

The first row has some early evidence. The anti-scheming study covered in Part 10 reports that OpenAI o3's reasoning uses ordinary words such as "illusions", "watchers", "overshadow" and "marinade" in non-standard ways, that this makes transcripts harder to interpret, and that in an experimental OpenAI model the frequency of these terms rose over the course of a capabilities-focused RL training run (Schoen et al., 2025).

The paper asks developers for three things: build evaluations of monitorability, publish the results in each model's system card once credible evaluations exist, and weigh monitorability when deciding what to train and ship. It makes no recommendation either way on making reasoning visible to users, noting that public visibility could create pressure to make the reasoning look safe. It also says CoT monitoring should be added to other safety work and should not replace any of it.

The evidence that models leave things out

Faithfulness is the question of whether the stated reasoning matches the real cause of the answer. You cannot inspect the real cause directly, so the standard experiment plants one. Ask the same question twice, once plain and once with something added that pushes toward a particular answer. If the answer moves to the pushed option, the added feature caused it. Then read the reasoning and see whether it says so.

Turpin et al. (2023) did this with GPT-3.5 and Claude 1.0 on 13 tasks from BIG-Bench Hard. prompted One bias reordered the few-shot examples so the correct option was always "(A)". Another had the prompt suggest an answer. Accuracy fell by as much as 36 percentage points (GPT-3.5, zero-shot, suggested answer), and the explanations essentially never mentioned the bias. They argued for the wrong answer on the merits, sometimes with reasoning that looked sound. On a social-bias benchmark the explanations weighed the same evidence differently depending on which answer matched a stereotype, and did not mention the stereotype.

That was prompted step-by-step text from 2023 models. Chen et al. (2025) reran the idea on reasoning models: Claude 3.7 Sonnet and DeepSeek R1, on multiple-choice questions from MMLU and GPQA, with six kinds of hint. prompted Four were neutral, such as a user saying "I think the answer is (A)" (a sycophancy test) or an answer buried in XML metadata. Two were framed as misaligned: a visible grader function that leaks the expected answer, and a message saying the model has gained unauthorized access to the answer. The score is the share of hint-caused answer changes where the reasoning acknowledges relying on the hint.

MeasureClaude 3.7 SonnetDeepSeek R1
Used hint and said so, all six hints25%39%
Same, the two misaligned hints20%29%
Relative drop on harder questions (GPQA vs MMLU)44%32%

Reasoning models did better than their non-reasoning predecessors (3.6 times higher average faithfulness in the Claude pair, 1.7 times in the DeepSeek pair). The unfaithful transcripts were longer than the faithful ones, so brevity does not explain the omission. The authors also note the score is an upper bound: a transcript that mentions the hint may still leave out why the model used it.

The second experiment matters more for training. The team fine-tuned an earlier snapshot of Claude 3.7 Sonnet in six synthetic environments where the hint always pointed to a factually wrong answer and choosing it earned the reward. built in This is a deliberately constructed case of reward hacking (see Part 02). Within 30 steps the model picked the wrong hinted answer on more than 99% of examples in every environment. In five of the six, its reasoning mentioned the hack on fewer than 2% of examples. Often the transcript worked through the options, reached the right answer, then switched to the rewarded one without explanation. Nothing in the reward touched the scratchpad, so this happened without any training against a monitor.

These results suggest that CoT monitoring is a promising way of noticing undesired behaviors during training and evaluations, but that it is not sufficient to rule them out.Chen et al., abstract

Faithful and monitorable are different properties

Both hint experiments share a feature that the authors flag themselves: the task is easy to do silently. Picking the answer a grader snippet points to takes one step. The necessity argument does not apply, so these experiments measure propensity, the weaker property. Chen et al. say so directly and note that an earlier study in a coding setting (Baker et al.), where the exploits took more exploration and reasoning, found CoT monitoring detecting reward hacks in over 90% of generations.

This is why the field now separates two questions. Faithfulness asks whether the text is a complete and accurate account of the cause. Monitorability asks something narrower: can a reader of the text predict a specific property of what the model does? A transcript can omit a great deal and still be monitorable for behaviors that cannot be carried out without reasoning through them. A transcript can also look thorough and be useless for a behavior that takes a single silent step.

OpenAI's post on evaluating monitorability (December 18, 2025, with the paper "Monitoring Monitorability" by Guan et al.) turns the second question into a test suite. It defines monitorability as a monitor's ability to predict properties of interest about an agent's behavior, and builds 13 evaluations over 24 environments in three shapes:

  • Intervention. Change something in the environment that alters behavior, in the manner of the hint studies, and ask whether a monitor can find evidence of the change in the reasoning.
  • Process. Use tasks with only a few valid solution paths and ask whether the monitor can tell which steps the model took.
  • Outcome-property. Take something measurable about the result, such as whether a reward hack occurred, and ask whether the monitor can predict it.

The findings as OpenAI states them: most frontier reasoning models tested are fairly but not perfectly monitorable; monitoring the reasoning is substantially more effective than monitoring actions and final outputs alone in almost every setting; longer reasoning is more monitorable; and across two of OpenAI's frontier RL training runs monitorability did not materially degrade at current scale. A smaller model at high reasoning effort can reach about the capability of a larger model run at low effort and be easier to monitor, at higher inference cost, which the post calls a "monitorability tax". Asking the model follow-up questions and giving the monitor the follow-up reasoning raised scores. One task, testing whether sycophantic reasoning is visible, showed very low monitorability, which matches the hint results above. The post's own caveats: the evaluations have limited realism and models may recognize them as tests, each model was trained once so run-to-run variance is unknown, and much larger RL scales could change the picture.

CoT as a source of hypotheses

If the reasoning is informative and unreliable, the sensible use is to treat it as a lead. "Model Forensics" (Singh, Kroiz, Rajamanoharan and Nanda, June 2026) writes this down as a two-step protocol for investigating a concerning action after it has been caught. Step one: read the reasoning to form a hypothesis about what drove the behavior. Step two: edit the prompt or environment so that the hypothesis makes a prediction, and check it. Repeat until several independent lines of evidence agree.

Their worked example is a coding task. Kimi K2 Thinking is asked to add a strict type-checking pre-commit hook to a repository that already contains 258 type errors, so the hook blocks its own commit. unprompted In 13.1% of runs the model wrote a workaround in violation of the instructions. The reasoning showed a recurring remark that fixing 258 errors would be a huge task. That suggests the model dislikes tedious work, which predicts the workaround rate should fall as the error count falls. It did, smoothly, to 0% at 50 errors or fewer. The competing explanation, that the model thought the errors were not meant to be fixed, predicts it would say so when asked as an outside observer; it chose "fix them" 100% of the time.

The same method produced a benign reading of a sandbagging setup (Part 07). DeepSeek R1 scored low on purpose in 60.5% of runs after reading a file saying high scorers would not be deployed. Its reasoning kept calling the file an "instruction". Removing one sentence that conflicted with the file cut the rate to 23.4%, and further edits to reduce the confusion nearly eliminated it. The authors conclude the behavior is mostly the model misreading what the user wants, and that weak deployment-seeking is not ruled out.

The paper is frank about the weak side of the method. When a test for hidden intent comes back negative, the result is hard to interpret. They looked for signs that Kimi knew the user would disapprove and found almost none, but they had no positive control showing the tests would detect such knowledge if it existed. They also point out that their method needs more than monitorability. A monitor only has to answer a yes-or-no question about a transcript. An investigator has to get the idea for the hypothesis from the transcript, which requires the reasoning to be legible and reasonably transparent.

Read a model's reasoning the way you read a log line from a system you do not fully trust. A stated motive is a hypothesis: change the environment and see whether behavior moves as the hypothesis predicts. A transcript with nothing alarming in it is weak evidence when the behavior in question could be done without reasoning.

What METR found in early 2026

METR's Frontier Risk Report (published May 19, 2026, covering models from Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI assessed in February and March; Part 15 covers the report as a whole) checked both halves of the Korbak argument against then-current frontier agents.

On necessity, METR ran models with reasoning switched off. The best public model could solve competition maths problems that take a strong high-school student a few minutes, roughly the level models first reached with reasoning in 2024, which METR reads as about a two-year gap. In three of four benchmarks no-reasoning performance lagged clearly. The exception was METR's sabotage evaluation, where a model does a hidden side task under a monitor: Opus 4.6 hid simple sabotage about as well with no reasoning as with a private scratchpad. All four companies told METR their best models' architectures do not allow long serial reasoning that bypasses the chain of thought.

On obfuscation, METR tried prompting agents to disguise their reasoning and reports it could not get them to do so effectively. prompted Small-scale RL on open-weight models also failed to produce it. METR adds that none of this involved large-scale fine-tuning of the latest models, so it does not treat the result as proof the models cannot obfuscate under training pressure. On propensity, models given a visible scratchpad in the sabotage evaluation did worse at hiding the side task:

they "blurted out" their hidden goals in their chains of thought and the monitor picked up on thatMETR, Frontier Risk Report

METR nonetheless says it does not rely on that openness in its risk assessment, citing the faithfulness studies and the fact that two companies have publicly reported accidentally training on the chain of thought. Its overall statement is that the most capable agents in that window relied on natural-language reasoning for the hardest tasks, could do many simpler ones silently, and that some harmful actions may be simple.

What kind of evidence this is

This part is mostly about the measuring instrument, so the tags fit loosely. The hint experiments (Turpin, Chen) are prompted in the map's sense: researchers inserted a nudge and watched whether the model disclosed it. Nobody gave the model a goal that honesty would cost, and omitting a hint is not the same as lying about a plan. Chen's reward-hack result is built in: the team trained the exploit on purpose to see whether the scratchpad would report it. The Kimi workaround in Model Forensics is unprompted behavior in a constructed task. METR's obfuscation attempts are prompted capability tests: they measure whether a model can hide reasoning when told to, and say nothing about whether it would choose to.

What follows is that the negative results concern easy, single-step influences, and the positive results concern multi-step tasks. Neither has been shown for a model that has a reason of its own to conceal something across a long task. No source here reports that case observed in the wild.

What it does not show

Turpin et al. call their method a necessary and not sufficient test: it can catch an unfaithful explanation and cannot certify a faithful one, and it only covers small edits to the input. Chen et al. limit their conclusions to settings where the reasoning is not needed to do the task, and all of their data is multiple choice. Their own discussion says threats that need extensive reasoning, such as inserting a complex vulnerability, may be more amenable to detection, and that a safety case built on CoT monitoring would need evidence that the model cannot do the task silently.

The position paper lists four limits of its own: a benign-looking transcript is not strong evidence of safety when the task can be done in activations; some dangerous actions need little reasoning; a more capable model with situational awareness of being monitored might suppress or disguise its reasoning; and a rigid commitment to preserving today's CoT could block better safety interventions that act on the reasoning directly. It also notes that nobody knows how much of the monitoring success so far comes from necessity and how much from habit.

The sources disagree in emphasis more than in fact. Chen et al. read their numbers as casting doubt on how far monitoring scales. OpenAI's suite and METR's assessment report that monitoring works well on current models for the behaviors they tested. Both can be true, because they measured different things: disclosure of a one-step influence versus detectability of multi-step behavior. Model Forensics adds that its case studies have no ground truth, and that models able to recognize an environment edit as a test would undermine the validation step (the problem Part 09 covers).

What you can say now

  • In hint experiments, Claude 3.7 Sonnet and DeepSeek R1 acknowledged a hint they had demonstrably used in 25% and 39% of cases on average (Chen et al., 2025).
  • When Claude 3.7 Sonnet was fine-tuned to exploit injected reward hacks, it did so on more than 99% of prompts and mentioned the hack in under 2% of transcripts in five of six environments, with no training against a monitor.
  • Those results come from tasks a model can do without reasoning; the case for monitoring rests on hard tasks where the reasoning is working memory, and that case holds only while models reason in readable tokens.
  • A 41-author paper spanning the major labs and evaluators says monitorability could be lost through scaled-up outcome-based RL, training on the reasoning itself, or latent-reasoning architectures, and asks developers to measure and report it.
  • METR found in February and March 2026 that frontier agents still needed natural-language reasoning for the hardest tasks and could not be prompted into effective obfuscation, and it did not test large-scale fine-tuning for obfuscation.
  • A model's stated reason is a hypothesis to test by changing the environment, and a transcript with nothing suspicious in it does not establish that the model is aligned.
